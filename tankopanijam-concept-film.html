Concept film · 90 seconds · internal buy-in

The Brief
Script & Prompt Pack

A film of an event that hasn't happened yet, made to get the room to feel it before they fund it. Script first — it works read aloud with no footage at all. Generator prompts after, shot by shot.

Runtime ~90 sec
Shots 13
Dialogue None
Patient faces Never shown

The one creative decision that makes this work

You never see a patient's face. Not once. Hands, a shoulder, a blanket, the back of a head, a controller being passed. The camera stays on the students watching, and their faces carry the whole emotional load.

This is the better film. The reaction shot has always been stronger than the subject — you don't watch the gift, you watch the person opening it. Withholding the face makes the audience lean in and finish the image themselves, and what they imagine will be more affecting than anything a generator produces.

It also solves three real problems at once. Synthetic children in medical settings is where AI video generators get uncanny and where they refuse outright. Showing hospital leadership fabricated footage of "patients" invites exactly the reaction you don't want from Legal, Comms, and Child Life. And a concept film that respects patient dignity in its own construction is itself an argument that you'll run the real event carefully.

Sequencing: show this to Child Life and Communications before leadership. Ten minutes, framed as "help me not screw this up." They become co-authors instead of people who found out afterward — and they're the two groups who could kill it on sight if it lands wrong.

Before the film: the script works alone

You asked whether the script by itself could do the job. It can, and in some rooms it's the stronger play — reading it aloud costs nothing, can't misfire, and puts the images in people's heads where they're always better.

If you open a meeting cold by reading the beats below with no preamble, you'll get most of the effect. The film is for the rooms you're not in.

April 4–9, 2027 · Nemours Estate, Wilmington, DE

First one.

Read-aloud version

For opening a meeting cold, with no screen. Read it slowly. Don't explain it first, and don't explain it after — let the room speak before you do.

Cold open · ~75 seconds spoken

A hundred years ago, someone filled a house with everything they loved. Then locked the door. Four miles away, a kid is waiting. Waiting is the part nobody makes a game about. So we asked them to write the brief. Five college teams. Seventy-two hours. Build it out of that house — its objects, its history, its music. Day two is when it breaks. It always does. Day three, they hand in a hard drive. Day four, we give it back to them. A controller goes from an adult's hand into a much smaller one. And a kid leans forward and points at the screen, because something on it is theirs. They said what would help, and somebody built it. We don't watch the kid. We watch the students watching the kid. That's the event. We're doing it in April. I'd like you in it.

Generator prompts

How to run this

Workflow

  • Prepend the style block to every shot prompt. Consistency across clips is the hardest part of AI video and a fixed style header is most of the solution.
  • Generate 3–4 variants per shot. Expect to use one in four. Thirteen shots means budgeting for roughly fifty generations.
  • Clips run 5–10 seconds. Cut them down hard in the edit — most of these shots want 2–4 seconds on screen.
  • Add every title card in post. Generators still mangle text. Do them in Resolve, where you have full control over type and timing.

Two rules that keep it clean

  • Never prompt for a child's face. Describe hands, shoulders, the back of a head, a silhouette. If a generator returns a visible child's face, discard the clip — no exceptions, even if it's beautiful.
  • Never prompt for the hospital by name or brand. Generic clinical environment only. This is a concept film, not documentation, and it should not look like it depicts a real place.

Sound

Don't license anything for an internal concept piece. Generate an original score — you already work in Suno. What the film needs: sparse piano over a mechanical clock pulse for the first thirty seconds, a hard tempo break at Beat 06, strings entering at Beat 10 and resolving at 11. Ask for instrumental, no vocals, cinematic, ~100 seconds.

Style block — prepend to every shot

Style header

STYLE: Cinematic documentary. Shot on 35mm film, anamorphic, shallow depth of field, natural available light only. Muted desaturated palette. Fine film grain. Slight handheld motion, no camera shake. Realistic, restrained, unglamorous. No lens flare, no slow-motion, no drone shots. COLOR LOGIC (important, keep consistent): Archive/estate scenes = warm amber, tungsten, deep shadow Hospital scenes = cool blue-grey, clean, flat daylight Final atrium scenes = both temperatures in one frame FORMAT: 16:9, 24fps, 5-8 seconds. NEGATIVE: no on-screen text, no logos, no branding, no children's faces, no visible medical procedures, no blood, no distress, no stock-footage look, no corporate glossy lighting, no smiling-at-camera, no AI sheen.

Edit notes

The three cuts that carry it

  • Shot 03 → 04. Warm to cold, no transition. This is the whole premise in one frame change.
  • Shot 06 → 07. Silence to noise. Let Beat 05 sit a full beat longer than feels comfortable before you break it.
  • Shot 11 → 12. Cutting away from the screen to the students' faces. If you're tempted to cut back to show what they're seeing, don't — the restraint is the payoff.

Pacing

First half slow, second half accelerating, then a hard brake at Beat 11. The reaction shot should feel like the film stops moving. Two seconds longer than comfortable.

Type

Title cards in post. Simple, centered, generous margins, hold long enough to read twice. Keep them quiet — the words are doing enough work without help.

How to use it in the room

Say nothing first. No setup, no context, no "so I put together a little concept piece." Dim the lights and play it. The instinct to pre-explain is the instinct that kills it.

Then one sentence: "None of that happened yet. It happens in April, and I need your help to make it real." Stop talking. Let them fill the silence — whoever speaks first is your strongest ally in that room, and you've just found out who.

Label it honestly on the file itself. Concept film, illustrative, not documentation, no real patients depicted. Put it in the first frame or the file name. That single line is the difference between a bold pitch and a problem — and it costs you nothing, because everyone in the room already knows the event hasn't happened.
